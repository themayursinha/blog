In August 1955, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon proposed a summer research project at Dartmouth.

Their conjecture was audacious: every feature of learning or intelligence might be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it. They wanted computers to use language, form abstractions, solve problems associated with human thought, and improve themselves.

The proposal gave the project a name: *artificial intelligence*.

Seven decades later, those two words still shape what we think the machines are, what we fear they may become, and what companies can claim on their behalf.

The field acquired a theory of itself through its name before science had acquired a stable definition of intelligence.

## A deliberate name

The popular story says McCarthy replaced the dull phrase *automata studies* with something more evocative because the earlier label attracted little attention. A passage I saved from Karen Hao’s *Empire of AI* describes the new name as a marketing tool.

The primary record supports a more careful version.

McCarthy had been disappointed that work gathered under automata studies focused on mathematical automata rather than the machine reasoning he wanted to pursue. “Artificial intelligence” made the desired research agenda explicit. The phrase drew a boundary around a more ambitious field.

It was a naming decision with intellectual and institutional consequences. Whether we call it marketing depends on how broadly we use the word. The name certainly helped a group of ideas cohere, attract researchers, and promise a destination.

Names do this. They organize attention before they describe reality perfectly.

## Intelligence carries human baggage

Call a system a statistical prediction engine and people ask about accuracy.

Call it artificial intelligence and they ask whether it understands, wants, reasons, deceives, creates, or deserves rights.

The second label imports a dense cluster of human meanings. Intelligence is associated with judgment, comprehension, adaptability, agency, and status. A machine that performs well on a benchmark begins to inherit qualities the benchmark never measured.

This is partly why arguments about AI become arguments about humanity. A program writes a persuasive paragraph, and we debate whether creativity has been automated. A model solves a problem, and we wonder whether it reasoned. A chatbot speaks fluently, and users begin to infer a stable self behind the conversation.

The behavior is real. The interpretation travels farther.

## A field without one agreed object

Researchers can build useful systems without agreeing on a universal definition of intelligence. Engineering often advances through operational measures: performance on a task, generalization to new conditions, sample efficiency, planning depth, robustness, or the ability to use tools.

The difficulty begins when a local measure becomes a total claim.

Chess skill once served as a symbol of machine intelligence. Then computers became overwhelmingly strong at chess, and the achievement moved into the background. Image classification, language generation, coding, and scientific problem solving have followed similar paths. Each success expands capability while leaving the larger category contested.

There may be no single essence waiting beneath every intelligent behavior. Intelligence could be a family of capacities arranged differently across organisms and machines.

The name encourages us to seek one ladder. Reality may contain several dimensions.

## The security cost of anthropomorphism

As a security engineer, I care about the name because language changes trust.

People reveal more to a system that sounds understanding. They delegate more to an agent described as autonomous and capable. They may interpret a coherent explanation as evidence that the system inspected the right sources, remembered correctly, or followed a valid chain of reasoning.

Fluency acquires authority.

Attackers can exploit that authority. A model can be manipulated through poisoned context, deceptive instructions, compromised tools, or carefully framed goals. The resulting output may still sound like the work of an intelligent and conscientious colleague.

Security cannot depend on deciding whether the system truly understands. It must govern observable capabilities: what information the system can access, what actions it can take, which claims can be traced to evidence, and where human authorization remains necessary.

The name should never become a control.

## Language also limits imagination

Anthropomorphic language can exaggerate a machine. It can also make us underestimate unfamiliar forms of computation.

If intelligence must resemble human reasoning, we may overlook systems that solve problems through processes no person would use or understand intuitively. We keep asking whether the machine thinks like us when its value may lie in exploring patterns that do not fit human habits.

The Dartmouth proposal itself was broader than imitation. It asked how machines might use language, form concepts, solve problems, and improve. Those goals could produce abilities that resemble human cognition in some places and diverge sharply in others.

Artificial intelligence may eventually look least intelligent when judged through a human metaphor.

## Keeping the name, loosening its spell

The term is too established to replace, and replacement would solve little. A new phrase would gather its own mythology.

The better response is precision.

When we say a system is intelligent, we should specify the capability. Can it plan across unfamiliar conditions? Can it revise a belief after contradictory evidence? Can it distinguish uncertainty from absence? Can it act reliably when an attacker controls part of its context? Can a human reconstruct why it made a consequential choice?

These questions turn a grand noun into testable properties.

The name *artificial intelligence* helped create a field because it pointed toward an extraordinary ambition. Its success also made the phrase available to every product, prediction, and fear that wanted to borrow some of that ambition.

We can keep the name while refusing to let it finish the argument.

The machine should be judged by what it can do, how it fails, and which powers we place behind its interface. Intelligence is the beginning of the inquiry, not the verdict.
