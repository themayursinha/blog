I found an old note about the *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* while searching through years of abandoned plans, copied passages, unfinished questions, and book highlights.

The note divides knowledge into two broad kinds. Physics studies matter. Biology studies life. Astronomy studies stars. Each discipline takes something as an object and tries to understand it.

Then the note turns toward a different inquiry: *adhyatma vidya*, the study of the Self. Its object, if that word can still be used, is the subject through whom every other object becomes known.

The distinction felt newly alive beside modern AI. We have built machines that can explain a black hole, summarize a philosophy, inspect a software system, compare arguments, and produce a careful answer before a human reader has fully formed the question. Our capacity to obtain answers has changed faster than our understanding of the person receiving them.

The Upanishadic question remains in the room:

Who is asking?

## Knowledge usually points outward

Almost every instrument extends our reach into the world.

A telescope lets the eye travel farther. A microscope brings hidden structure into view. A database remembers more than one mind can hold. A language model can cross a large field of text and return patterns, explanations, and connections with startling speed.

These tools differ enormously, yet they share an orientation. They help a subject know an object.

The subject is usually assumed. Science can measure the body, model the brain, correlate experience with neural activity, and study the conditions under which awareness changes. Those achievements matter. They have transformed vague questions into serious research.

Still, the first-person fact survives every description. There is experience, and experience is present to someone from within.

A complete account of the brain may eventually explain why a person reports pain, how attention selects a stimulus, and which neural processes accompany the recognition of a face. The account remains a description encountered by a conscious reader. The machinery appears as an object again.

The old inquiry asks us to notice the one position that cannot be placed in front of itself in quite the same way.

## AI sharpens the asymmetry

An AI system can produce an eloquent answer to “Who am I?” It can compare Advaita, Buddhism, neuroscience, psychoanalysis, and existentialism. It can ask follow-up questions and generate an exercise for self-inquiry.

The answer may be useful. It may even alter the reader’s life.

Yet the usefulness occurs on the reader’s side of the exchange. Words become insight, discomfort, recognition, or resistance inside a field of experience the answer can describe but cannot hand over.

This does not prove that machines lack consciousness. We do not possess a reliable test for subjective experience in an unfamiliar system. Confidence in either direction outruns the evidence.

The narrower point is enough. No answer can replace the first-person work of examining the one to whom the answer appears. A model may guide the inquiry, but the model cannot perform my side of it for me.

That boundary matters because AI encourages a subtle confusion between access and understanding. The answer arrives, so we feel that the question has been handled. In outward domains, this can already be misleading. A summary can be correct while the reader lacks the structure needed to judge it.

With self-knowledge, the gap becomes impossible to ignore. A paragraph about awareness is still an object within awareness.

## The limit may belong to the yardstick

Another note in my archive used an analogy that stayed with me. A chimpanzee cannot represent algebra in the way a trained human can. An ant has no concept of the internet. Their limits say little about what exists. They describe what a particular cognitive architecture can hold.

Human beings rarely apply the same humility to ourselves.

We expect a sufficiently advanced intelligence to explain itself in terms we can follow. Future systems may instead discover patterns that resist our intuitions and compress their reasons into accounts shaped for human comprehension. The explanation available to us may resemble a map drawn for a mind with fewer dimensions.

This possibility complicates the Upanishadic question. Perhaps the human subject is neither the summit of intelligence nor the final form of inwardness. Our first-person experience gives us privileged access to our own awareness, but it does not grant a complete theory of awareness itself.

The yardstick may fail in both directions. We may struggle to understand intelligence greater than ours and remain unable to grasp the ordinary fact of being the one who understands anything at all.

## The machine and the questioner

One of my older essays compared consciousness to music emerging from an orchestra. No instrument contains the melody. The organized activity gives rise to something perceived at another level.

That metaphor may be right. Consciousness may emerge from physical processes once they acquire the necessary organization. It may also leave something unexplained. A score describes relations among notes; it does not become the hearing of the music.

The honest position contains uncertainty. We know that damage to the brain can alter memory, language, personality, and perception. We also know that subjective experience is the condition through which every theory, measurement, and doubt becomes available to us.

AI does not settle the argument. It makes the missing piece harder to avoid.

We can now interact with systems that display remarkable linguistic and analytical competence. Their existence forces us to separate intelligence, consciousness, self-knowledge, and performance more carefully. A system may excel at one while leaving the others unresolved.

The phrase “artificial intelligence” once encouraged those concepts to travel together. Experience is teaching us to pull them apart.

## A question that changes the order of things

I still want the outward answers. I want better tools for physics, medicine, engineering, history, and every field where human attention is scarce. Refusing those tools would turn philosophy into nostalgia.

The inward question changes their place rather than reducing their value.

An answer can inform me. It can reveal an error, offer language for an intuition, or show a path I had missed. It cannot become the act of seeing on my behalf. Somewhere, understanding must occur as lived recognition rather than received text.

This is why the old note no longer feels like material stored on a philosophy shelf. We are entering an age of abundant answers. The scarcity may move toward judgment, attention, and the difficult intimacy of knowing what an answer means in a life.

The machines will become better at telling us about the world. They may become better at telling us about ourselves. They may even force us to widen the category of the self.

Through all of that, one inquiry remains irreducibly close.

Before asking what the machine knows, notice the one for whom knowledge matters.
