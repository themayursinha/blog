We like to say that the system is broken. The promotion process is broken, the company is broken, the market is broken. The people making decisions cannot see the obvious inefficiency, so the conscientious person responds in the way conscientious people often do: by working harder.

More hours. More ownership. More emergencies absorbed before anyone else has to notice them.

The less comfortable possibility is that the system is working exactly as its incentives demand.

That possibility changes the diagnosis. A system can be destructive to you and still be functioning perfectly for the people who benefit from it. The friction you experience may not be a defect waiting for your heroic intervention. It may be the mechanism through which your surplus effort is extracted.

If so, working harder simply feeds additional input into the same arrangement.

## The self-inflicted attack

In a denial-of-service attack, a machine receives more work than it can process. Latency rises. Useful requests are crowded out. Eventually, the system degrades or stops responding.

Nobody looks at the collapsed server and praises its work ethic.

Human beings routinely do something similar to themselves. We flood our own attention with meetings, messages, deadlines, rescued projects, and invisible emotional labor. We overclock the mind, suppress the warnings, and call the resulting heat ambition.

The organization may indeed become more productive. I have seen technically strong people keep a failing process alive for years, only to discover that their reliability removed any incentive to repair it. The critical question is who captures the increase.

If every improvement in your speed merely raises the amount of work routed toward you, you have not gained leverage. You have trained the system to treat your remaining capacity as an available resource.

This is what I mean by a self-inflicted DDoS: exhausting your cognitive infrastructure to maintain a topology you do not own.

## Effort has an exchange rate

Hard work is not automatically converted into justice.

It is converted according to the exchange rate of the system in which it occurs.

If an institution rewards visibility over substance, your substance may become someone else's visibility. If it rewards proximity to capital over technical correctness, being right will not be enough. If it rewards the people who conceal structural failure, repeatedly rescuing the system may make the failure harder to see.

None of this means that every employer is malicious or every manager is plotting extraction. Institutions are rarely that coherent. Good people can reward excellent work, and sustained effort can create skill, trust, and opportunity.

The narrower claim is that effort and reward are connected by structure, not morality. Before deciding that more effort is virtuous, you need to understand the structure converting it into outcomes.

## Map the game you are actually playing

Most career advice describes an ideal game: perform well, become valuable, earn trust, and advance.

Real organizations add budgets, title bands, political exceptions, hiring freezes, executive narratives, and managers whose incentives only partially overlap with yours. The institution owns the ladder, including its rungs, timing, and definition of impact.

Before increasing effort, ask:

- Who owns the scarce resource?
- Who receives the upside when this problem is solved?
- Does exceptional performance change my authority, compensation, or options?
- Am I removing a bottleneck, or becoming the permanent mechanism that compensates for it?
- What behavior does the institution reward when nobody is speaking aspirationally?

These questions separate the stated rules from the executable ones. Cynicism would be refusing to look.

## Why load rarely becomes leverage

Early in a career, taking on difficult work can be transformative. Competence is built by touching real systems under real constraints. Being reliable matters.

But reliability has a dangerous final form. You become infrastructure.

You are always called when something breaks. You know the systems nobody documented. You absorb ambiguity so effectively that leadership never feels its cost. Everyone agrees that you are indispensable, yet your conditions barely change.

Being indispensable can feel like leverage, but without bargaining power it often means that you have become a critical dependency owned by someone else.

Leverage is different. It may come from a scarce skill, an asset, a reputation, a distribution channel, a decision point, a product, or a repeatable service that does not require a linear increase in exhaustion. It moves you from *I can perform this work* toward *I control a scarce path to this outcome*.

That does not require becoming an entrepreneur or treating ordinary employment with contempt. A good job can provide income, learning, community, reputation, and exposure to consequential problems.

The mistake is allowing its internal reward system to define every form your value is permitted to take.

## Sell the collapse of uncertainty

Time is convenient to buy because it is capped.

If someone purchases five hours of your labor, the transaction has a natural boundary. But five hours from a beginner and five hours from a person with fifteen years of judgment are not the same economic object.

An experienced architect may prevent a launch failure in an afternoon because the relevant patterns have become visible to them. Their value lies in collapsing uncertainty: avoiding the outage, protecting the revenue, and making the path safe.

Whenever possible, align the exchange with that outcome rather than with the number of hours your nervous system spent producing it.

This principle applies beyond consulting. A reusable tool, a public body of work, a course, a trusted niche, or a system that continues producing value when you are asleep can all decouple output from personal depletion.

The goal is a less fragile relationship between value and exhaustion, free from the internet fantasy of effortless passive income. Becoming better at the work should eventually create leverage instead of merely attracting more work.

## Burnout as observability

We often treat burnout as a personal failure of discipline, resilience, sleep, or attitude.

Sometimes those things matter. Sometimes the person needs rest, boundaries, medical support, or a different way of working.

Burnout can also be observability finally catching up with bad architecture.

Your body may be reporting what the organizational dashboard excludes: the current protocol is dropping packets. More discipline can keep the node alive a little longer. It cannot repair the topology.

The useful questions are therefore structural. Examine what you are optimizing for, who captures the upside, and whether the work expands your future options. Then ask what would have to change before additional effort produced additional leverage.

## Work where intensity compounds

Laziness, detachment, and pure transactionality solve little.

It is to reserve intensity for places where intensity compounds: skills that improve your bargaining power, relationships built on reciprocal trust, problems whose solution changes your future, and assets you can continue to own.

Work hard, be generous, and rescue things worth rescuing. But study the routing table while you do it. Build enough leverage that some of your effort changes your own state instead of turning you into the hardest-working node in a network that consumes every spare cycle.
