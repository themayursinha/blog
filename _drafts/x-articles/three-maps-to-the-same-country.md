I recently opened a strategy document from an earlier version of myself and experienced the physical sensation of embarrassment.

The language was enormous. There were phases, doctrines, codenames, sovereign infrastructure, world-changing systems, and absolute confidence about technologies I had not yet built. The document read like a military campaign written by someone whose immediate task was still an experiment on a laptop.

My first impulse was to close it and hope the archive would forget.

Instead, I kept reading.

Beneath the grandiosity, I found a pattern. This was the first of three ambitious maps I had drawn over several years. The names changed. The imagined products changed. The route changed completely. All three were trying to reach the same country.

I wanted to build autonomous systems without surrendering control over what they could become.

## The first map: intelligence as leverage

The earliest plan began with finance.

I imagined a quantitative system that could turn patterns in markets into capital for more ambitious research. The financial engine would fund independent infrastructure. Independence would fund a new kind of AI. The AI would eventually become a platform for solving problems at a scale ordinary companies could not reach.

Almost every link in this chain was speculative.

The plan confused a sequence of possibilities with a strategy. It treated difficult businesses as steps in a staircase, as if success in one would naturally unlock competence in the next. It underestimated regulation, capital, data, operations, research risk, and time.

Yet the document already contained concerns that still occupy me: dependence on external infrastructure, concentration of capability, the security of sensitive data, and the tension between useful intelligence and uncontrolled power.

The map was poor. The destination was visible.

## The second map: philosophy as architecture

Another plan tried to build an AI architecture from ideas in Vedanta.

The proposal was seduced by conceptual symmetry. Consciousness, causality, karma, memory, and agency seemed to offer an alternative vocabulary for machine intelligence. Western AI, the document declared, had chosen brute-force statistical correlation. A philosophically grounded architecture would take a more elegant path.

I still think philosophy can expose assumptions hidden inside technical language. It can help us ask what we mean by agency, selfhood, knowledge, intention, and responsibility.

But a philosophical vocabulary does not become a computational architecture through enthusiasm. A metaphor is not an algorithm. Civilizational pride is not an evaluation method. Elegance claimed before implementation is branding.

This second map taught me something the first had missed. A serious bridge between domains must carry weight in both directions. Philosophy should sharpen the technical specification. Technical results should be allowed to embarrass the philosophy.

Without that discipline, synthesis becomes decoration.

## The third map: security before intelligence

The next plan moved closer to my actual experience.

Instead of inventing a new intelligence from first principles, I began thinking about how existing AI systems would fail in real organizations. Agents would receive credentials, call tools, read private context, remember information, and take actions whose consequences extended beyond a chat window.

The question became concrete: how do we let a system act without trusting its intentions, explanations, or internal state as the final authority?

That question belongs to security engineering.

Security assumes that useful components can fail, that identities and intentions can be misrepresented, and that boundaries must survive mistakes. It asks about reachability, authority, evidence, and containment. These are less glamorous words than artificial general intelligence. They are also closer to something that can be built and tested.

The third map covered less territory. For the first time, the scale matched the tools in my hands.

## Why embarrassing plans matter

Old vision documents are easy to mock because they preserve confidence after reality has removed its supporting assumptions.

Deleting them would destroy useful evidence.

They reveal recurring motives before those motives learn professional language. In my case, every plan returned to autonomy, infrastructure, concentrated intelligence, and the need for guardrails. The ideas wandered because the underlying concern had not yet found a tractable form.

Grandiosity acted as a crude signal.

This does not make the plans secretly correct. Most of their claims were wrong. Some were inflated by the style of the AI systems I used to help write them. The language exceeded my judgment, and I mistook coherence for feasibility.

Still, embarrassment contains information. We cringe because our standards have improved. The distance between the document and the reader measures learning.

## From destiny to stages

The plans that failed described destiny. The work that succeeded had stages.

In my career, progress usually came through concrete structures: an application, an interview, an exam, a project with owners, a deadline, evidence, and a decision. The archive contains many beautiful plans that never acquired those properties.

A vision becomes useful when it can survive contact with a next step small enough to fail honestly.

That step needs an observable result. It should eliminate an assumption, produce an artifact, attract a real user, or reveal that the direction is wrong. The outcome must be allowed to revise the map.

This sounds less heroic than a five-phase campaign. It is also how serious work compounds.

## The country behind the maps

I no longer want to build the systems described in those documents. Their certainty now feels alien, and some of their ambitions deserve to remain in the archive.

The persistent question still feels like mine.

How can powerful autonomous systems remain useful without becoming ungovernable? How do we preserve human authority when software can interpret, decide, remember, and act? Which controls must exist outside the intelligence they constrain?

The current work is narrower than the old visions. It proceeds through specifications, experiments, failures, and boundaries. It makes fewer promises.

That narrowing once felt like a retreat. I now see it as progress.

Young ideas often arrive wearing costumes borrowed from empires, revolutions, and destiny. Maturity removes the costume and asks what can be demonstrated by Friday.

The three maps did not show me the route. They showed me which direction I kept facing.

Sometimes that is the only part of an old plan worth keeping.
