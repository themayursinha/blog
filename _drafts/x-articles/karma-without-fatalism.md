An old note in my archive asks a question I had apparently refused to settle: are karma and maya real?

Under the title, I had written five objections. Karma can sound like predestination. It can turn suffering into evidence of guilt from a life nobody remembers. It can excuse inequality, weaken social responsibility, and ask for belief in a mechanism that cannot be tested.

Those objections still trouble me.

The word *karma* is often used as if the universe keeps a hidden ledger. A good act produces a future credit. A cruel act creates a debt. When fortune arrives, the account is paying out. When somebody suffers, an invisible balance is supposedly being corrected.

This picture gives moral order to a world that often looks morally indifferent. It also creates a brutal temptation: treating another person’s pain as proof that they deserved it.

Any interpretation that makes compassion less urgent has lost something essential.

## The danger of explaining suffering backward

Imagine meeting someone born into poverty, violence, illness, or discrimination. A theory of past-life consequence can place the explanation inside the sufferer. Their present condition becomes the result of an action no one can inspect.

The theory is impossible to disprove because every absence of evidence belongs to a forgotten life. Worse, it can transform privilege into moral vanity. The fortunate person appears to have earned comfort before birth, while the unfortunate person receives a metaphysical lecture instead of help.

History shows how easily spiritual language can settle around existing power. A hierarchy survives longer when it presents itself as cosmic order.

This is where skepticism becomes an ethical duty. We cannot infer a person’s moral history from their circumstances. We cannot use karma to close an investigation into injustice. Whatever the doctrine means, it cannot relieve us of responsibility toward suffering visible now.

## Action leaves a residue

There is another way to approach karma that requires less metaphysical certainty.

Actions change the world, but they also change the actor.

Every repeated choice trains perception. Anger practiced often becomes easier to access. Evasion builds the reflex to evade again. Attention strengthens the capacity to attend. Generosity alters what we notice about other people. Courage lowers the threshold for the next courageous act.

The consequence may appear in the environment, the nervous system, a relationship, a reputation, or the range of choices that remain available later.

This reading does not exhaust the traditional meanings of karma. Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions develop the concept in different ways, often across rebirth and liberation. Reducing all of that to modern habit psychology would be intellectually dishonest.

Still, the practical layer deserves attention. What we repeatedly do becomes part of the machinery through which we meet the next moment.

Karma, viewed from here, describes accumulated direction.

## Freedom inside causation

The objection from predestination assumes that causes eliminate agency. If the present emerged from the past, perhaps every choice has already been made for us.

But agency has never required a person to appear without history.

We inherit a body, language, family, temperament, culture, and economic position. We carry habits formed before we understood what a habit was. Some constraints are intimate; others are political and material. Freedom begins inside this unequal field.

The current moment contains causes we did not choose and an action whose consequences are still open.

That is a modest freedom. It cannot erase history. It can alter a trajectory.

The distinction matters. Fatalism says the past owns the future. A causal view says the next action joins the causes from which the future will emerge.

Responsibility becomes possible without pretending that everyone begins from the same place.

## Maya does not make the world disposable

My old critique extended to *maya*. If the world is called an illusion, why take suffering, duty, or justice seriously?

The English word *illusion* causes trouble here. It suggests that the world is fake, like a stage set that can be ignored once the trick is exposed. Advaita makes a subtler claim. Our ordinary experience mistakes separateness, permanence, and independent existence for the final structure of reality.

The rope mistaken for a snake is the familiar example. Fear occurs because the rope is misperceived. Recognition changes the fear, but the rope does not disappear.

Likewise, questioning the ultimate status of the world cannot become permission to neglect life within it. Hunger still hurts. Violence still damages bodies and minds. A philosophy that sees one reality beneath apparent separation should intensify concern for other beings, because their suffering can no longer be dismissed as wholly external to oneself.

Metaphysics earns moral credibility through the conduct it produces.

## A forward-facing karma

I remain unconvinced by claims that present suffering reveals guilt from a previous birth. The evidence is inaccessible, and the social cost of the inference is too high.

I do find value in a forward-facing account of karma.

Every action enters a network of consequences. Some return through other people. Some become institutions. Some settle into character. Many remain unpredictable, and good people can suffer despite wise choices. Karma offers no guarantee that the world will distribute outcomes fairly on a human timetable.

Its practical demand is smaller and harder: act with attention because action continues after the moment in which it occurs.

That demand applies to individuals and systems. A company builds karma through incentives that reward particular behavior. A society builds karma through laws, exclusions, and memories. A technical system builds karma whenever yesterday’s design becomes tomorrow’s constraint.

Consequences accumulate even when no cosmic accountant is watching.

## What the doctrine should never excuse

Karma should never be used to tell a victim why they deserved harm. It should never replace medicine, evidence, policy, or material assistance. It should never turn structural injustice into a private spiritual failure.

The doctrine becomes useful when directed toward one’s own responsibility and dangerous when used to pronounce judgment on another person’s suffering.

I cannot prove the metaphysical architecture behind karma. I can observe that actions form habits, habits shape perception, perception influences choice, and choices build environments that act on us in return.

That loop is powerful enough to deserve respect.

The past may explain part of this moment. It does not issue the final instruction.

What we do next becomes part of what the future will have to inherit.
